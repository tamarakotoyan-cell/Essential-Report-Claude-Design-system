Executive summary

The public thinks better of the sector than the sector expects, and worse of its own chances than the facts allow

Access to justice is understood as a basic right, and the legal assistance sector is viewed as an essential service. That is the starting position, and it is stronger than most people inside the sector assume.

While stakeholders believe the public views legal assistance negatively, the research found the opposite. Those who have used the service describe an overwhelmingly positive experience, and they give a human face to work that is otherwise abstract.

The gap sits elsewhere. The public significantly overestimates how far the service reaches, and is shocked when told the limits of it. Regional coverage is where that shock lands hardest.

Three narrative frames were tested. Fairness achieved the strongest overall support with both audiences, and it did so for different reasons in each — which is what makes it usable across a campaign rather than in one part of it.

Research design

Three phases, sequenced so that each one informed the next. The public phase ran first so the stakeholder conversations could be grounded in what people actually said, rather than in what the sector believed they would say.

Phase 1

Public

What?

Three-day online overtime discussion board with n=38 participants.

Why?

Exploratory qualitative research into existing attitudes, and into public support for more funding.

Who?

50/50 male and female. 50/50 split between people who receive Centrelink payments and people who do not. Mix of ages, states, incomes and household types.

Phase 2

Stakeholders

What?

Nine in-depth interviews of 45 to 60 minutes with people working in government or the legal sector.

Why?

To get feedback on the narrative frames and messages before they were refined.

Who?

A mix of executive, political advisory and media advisory positions.

Phase 3

Refinement

What?

One-day online board with nine stakeholders reviewing the refined messages.

Why?

To understand how to optimise each message for government and for the public.

Who?

Communications coordinators, communications directors, media advisors and leadership roles.

'Legal aid' has an extremely strong brand among the public, and is seen to cover all elements of legal representation

Legal Aid has strong brand and name recognition.

  • Almost everyone says they have heard of Legal Aid and knows that it provides free legal advice and assistance across a wide range of matters.
  • While they might be conflating Legal Aid with community legal centres, this helps demonstrate the leadership position it holds in the sector.

There is an assumption that if someone needs Legal Aid, they will get it.

  • The public does not have a deep understanding of how and who gets to access the service.
  • There is low awareness of means testing, and this results in many assuming the service is available to anyone unable to afford private legal fees.

There is shock that the service is only accessible to eight per cent of people — this makes people realise how limited it is

The eight per cent figure does more work than any other piece of evidence tested. It is specific, it is surprising, and it reframes the problem from a question of quality to a question of scale.

There are gender differences in how the public views the Family Court, but agreement that a speedy resolution matters

Women connect emotionally to the safety implications of inefficiency.

Women understand implicitly how the Family Court can protect women in domestic violence situations. Discussing it elicits a strong emotional response around safety, risk to women's lives and the protection of children.

Men are more instrumental when thinking about the same institution.

They tend to talk about efficiency, and about enabling family members to get on with their lives and achieve financial fairness. Safety is mentioned, but from a detached perspective.

However, both groups agree that when matters are held up in the courts this causes more harm to all parties, and that it is in everyone's benefit to have matters resolved quickly.

Fairness: stakeholders prefer this frame because it is relatable to the public and highlights the scale of the problem

Positive
  • Stakeholders believe this approach will gain traction with the public and resonate strongly, because fairness is a core part of the Australian identity.
  • The idea that access to justice is currently a privilege of the rich, and that many do not have access, feels credible and concerning.
  • The phrase “justice delayed is justice denied” is powerful and memorable.
  • The eight per cent statistic is impactful, helping people grasp the scale of the issue.
Improve
  • Some say to consider flipping the eight per cent statistic to put the emphasis on the limitation — that ninety-two per cent of people cannot access the service.
  • Some mention avoiding the implication that lawyers are motivated by money, as this risks undermining the message about fairness and service.

How the annotated narrative was marked up

Each frame was put in front of participants line by line. What follows is the Fairness narrative with the marks participants gave it.

Everyone deserves a fair go, and that has to include a fair go in court. Right now, legal help is something you can buy if you can afford it. Private representation runs to five hundred dollars an hour, and only eight per cent of Australians qualify for help. Justice delayed is justice denied — and lawyers should not be the ones who decide who gets it.

How the three frames compared

Three messages were tested with the public

Message frames

  • Fairness
  • Safety
  • Investment

Outcome

Fairness and Safety are the stronger of the three. The public feel that access to justice enabling everyone to have a fair go represents the fundamental value of the service. The Safety message is urgent and emotional, and gets a high level of support from women.

These were refined and tested with stakeholders

Message frames

  • Fairness
  • Safety
  • Access

Outcome

Fairness and Access are the stronger messages for stakeholders. They believe fairness will resonate with the public, and say the statistics presented are compelling. They also agree that highlighting the limitations of access frames the issue well.

Implications

Recommendations are directive. The client is paying for a position, not a survey of options.

The campaign needs to

  • Lead with the limit, not the service. Public goodwill is already high. The gap to close is the assumption that need equals access.
  • Keep the eight per cent, and give it its converse. Ninety-two per cent cannot access the service — that is the number that reframes the problem.
  • Use Fairness as the spine and Safety as the entry point for specific audiences. Fairness carries both audiences; Safety carries women more strongly than any other frame tested.
  • Give government tangible outcomes, not abstractions. Stakeholders were clear that ministers respond to measurable results rather than to the phrase “access to justice”.

What this research does not tell you.

This is qualitative work with n=38 public participants and eighteen stakeholder conversations. It establishes which arguments work and why. It does not size how many people hold each view. If the campaign needs a number to put in a media release, that is a separate quantitative wave.
